Does Money Buy Happiness?

Exploring the links between economic capacity, health outcomes, and happiness across 157 countries

Nations invest trillions in economic growth, yet GDP alone cannot explain why some countries thrive while others struggle. This interactive story explores how wealth, health, and social factors shape the well-being of populations around the world.

Wealth vs Happiness

GDP per capita shows a strong logarithmic relationship with happiness (r = 0.79), but the curve flattens above ~$30,000 — suggesting diminishing returns of wealth on well-being.

A Global Picture

Geography matters. Northern Europe and the Americas consistently rank highest, while Sub-Saharan Africa faces the greatest challenges across multiple well-being indicators.

Regional Comparison

Happiness varies dramatically between regions. Western Europe and North America lead, while Sub-Saharan Africa and Southern Asia lag behind — but there are outliers in every group.

How Has the World Changed?

Over three decades, global GDP has risen steadily, life expectancy has improved, and internet access has exploded — but not all countries have shared equally in this progress.

Life Expectancy vs Happiness

This scatter plot shows the relationship between life expectancy and happiness across countries. Although the trend line slopes upward, the relationship is weak. The correlation coefficient (r = 0.16) indicates a very weak positive relationship, while R² = 0.03 shows that life expectancy explains only 3% of the variation in happiness. We can therefore conclude that life expectancy in isolation is not a strong predictor of happiness. In contrast, the first scatter chart of GDP per capita vs happiness shows a strong non linear relationship between gdp and happiness which indicates diminishing returns at higher income levels. This shows that GDP is a much stronger predictor of happiness than life expectancy.

What Should Policymakers Do?

This data suggests that investment in healthcare, social safety nets, and civil liberties yields happiness returns that rival pure economic growth. Countries seeking to improve citizen well-being should pursue balanced development across all dimensions — not GDP alone.
